Learning in Public

The instinct is to wait until it is ready. Present the clean version, the finished version, the one with all the rough edges filed down. Then share it.

That instinct is understandable. And it is costing you something.

The researchers and builders I have watched grow the fastest have one habit in common. They share early. Not recklessly, not for the performance of it, but because they figured out that feedback you get at 40 percent done is worth ten times the feedback you get after you have submitted. By the time the work is finished, you are attached to it. You have already made the hard calls. You are less interested in hearing that a core assumption was shaky.

Early is when you can still actually use what people tell you.

There is a version of this that goes wrong, and it is worth naming. Sharing unfinished work is not the same as sharing sloppy work. The bar is not polish, but it is clarity. If someone cannot tell what you are trying to do, the feedback you get will be about that confusion, not about the thing you actually care about. So before you share, ask yourself one question: can I explain what this is trying to do in two sentences? If yes, it is ready to share. If not, spend another hour on that before you share the rest.

What it costs is real. You will sometimes be wrong in front of people. You will post something half-formed and someone will point out a flaw you should have caught. That is uncomfortable. It happened to me this year with a piece of analysis I put out before I had fully stress-tested it. Someone flagged a gap I had not considered, publicly and correctly, and I had to go back and revise.

That was one of the more useful things that happened to my research practice this year.

The gap would have made it into the final version otherwise. The public embarrassment was small. The course correction was significant. That is a trade I will keep taking.

What it gives back

People find you. This is the part that surprised me most. When you share work in progress, you surface for the exact people who are thinking about the same problems. They are not waiting for your polished paper. They are out there right now, stuck on something adjacent, and your half-formed idea is the thing that makes them reach out. Some of the most useful conversations I have had started with someone responding to something I shared before it was finished.

You also learn what you do not know faster. Writing something down, even informally, forces a kind of clarity that thinking alone never quite reaches. You realize the thing you thought you understood has a hole in it. That hole shows up in the writing before it shows up in the work. Sharing is just a more social version of that same forcing function.

And there is something else. The researchers who only share finished work tend to become less legible over time. You know they are working on something but you do not know what. Their thinking is invisible. The people who share as they go are building a record of how they think, not just what they concluded. That record compounds. A year of sharing in progress is worth more than a year of finished papers, at least for the people watching.

Starting small is the right move. You do not have to post a thread about your whole research direction. Share one result that surprised you. Ask one question you are genuinely stuck on. Write one paragraph about why a method you tried did not work. None of those require the work to be finished. All of them are useful to someone.

If you lead a team, this is also your call to make. The norm around sharing unfinished work comes from somewhere, and usually it comes from you. If your team sees you only presenting the polished version, they learn that unfinished means risky, that showing uncertainty is something to avoid. They will wait. And they will wait longer than they should, on everything.

Put something out before you are ready. Let the people thinking about the same things find you. Be wrong in public occasionally. The cost is smaller than it looks, and the return is not.
