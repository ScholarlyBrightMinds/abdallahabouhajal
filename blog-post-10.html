Saying I Do Not Know

The most confident person I have ever seen in a meeting was the one who said, halfway through a technical discussion, I am not sure I follow that. Can you walk me back through it?

The room did not lose respect for her. It did the opposite.

There is something that happens when you are surrounded by people who clearly know more than you about something. A small pressure builds. You start pattern-matching on terms you have heard before, nodding at the right pace, hoping the question lands on someone else. You tell yourself you will look it up later. And the conversation moves on, carrying assumptions you were not actually sure about, and now you are one layer further from the truth.

That is the bluff. It feels like survival. It is actually a slow leak.

In rooms where the stakes are real, where the decisions actually matter, bluffing is one of the most expensive things you can do. Not because someone will catch you, though they often do. Because you stop being useful. You cannot push back on something you did not really understand. You cannot ask the question that unlocks the room if you are too busy pretending you already knew the answer.

I have been in those rooms. As a researcher, often the youngest, sometimes the least experienced in a particular domain. The temptation to perform competence is real. But the times I have learned the most, and contributed the most, were the times I stopped performing and just said the thing.

I do not know what that term means. Can someone explain?

What I have come to believe is that admitting the gap is not a confession. It is a signal. It tells the room that you are tracking what is real rather than what sounds good. It tells the people who actually do know that you are worth explaining things to, because you are actually listening. And it almost always turns out that two or three other people in the room had the same question and were waiting for someone else to ask it.

You become the person who said what no one else would.

Here is the part for whoever is leading

If you are the most senior person in the room, this is not just about you. It is about what you make safe for everyone else.

People look to you to calibrate what is acceptable. If you bluff, they bluff. If you perform certainty you do not have, they learn that certainty is what gets rewarded here, and they start manufacturing it. Slowly, you build a room where nobody actually says what they think, because saying something uncertain feels like a risk.

That is when teams make bad decisions confidently.

The inverse is also true. When a leader says I do not know, let me find out, or I was wrong about that, it gives the whole room permission to be honest. That honesty is what surfaces the real problems early, before they become expensive. It is one of the most practical things you can do for a team, and it costs nothing except a moment of discomfort.

Say the thing you are not sure about. Out loud. Before someone else builds on it as if it were solid.

Not knowing something is not the gap. Pretending you do is.

· Abdallah

Currently in rooms where I know a lot about some things and very little about others, and trying to be honest about which is which.
